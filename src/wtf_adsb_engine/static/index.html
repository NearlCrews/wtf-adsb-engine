CURIOSITY, WITH CONTEXT

What makes the radar go WTF?

Every card comes from your receiver, saved replay archives or existing local metric history. These are spotting rules you can investigate, not proof of an aircraft’s mission or an incident.

Unusual airframes

Military and “interesting” database flags, a curated type watchlist, database years at least 50 years old, and unusual transmitted emitter categories remain searchable. Highlights favor ages 65+ and distinctive matches. Identity highlights have a 30-day repeat cooldown. The journal also notes the first type or address allocation country in this station's records and curated military and government variants. Metadata can be wrong and cannot establish a mission.

Rare in your sky

A type represented by at most 3 unique ICAO addresses and no more than 1% of known-type aircraft IDs in 30 days. A type must appear on at most 3 days and the individual aircraft on at most 2 days. This activates after 7 days with at least an hour of reception each, 24 total hours of recorded coverage, and 250 unique ICAO addresses. Valid compact replay intervals count toward coverage; imported metric maxima alone do not.

Big numbers

General spotting thresholds start at 600 kn and 49,000 ft. With at least 20 aircraft addresses, local type history adjusts these thresholds. They are spotting heuristics, not aircraft limits. Ordinary numeric highlights require four coherent updates over at least 30 seconds. Individual field ages remain unknown. Ground speed does not determine Mach number.

Odd data

A fresh-position jump exceeding 12 nm and implying more than 1,200 kn over 2–45 seconds; speeds over 900 kn; speeds or altitudes beyond a conservative envelope for the listed type; extreme reported altitudes or vertical rates; a 3,000+ ft gap between geometric and pressure altitude (4,000 ft from FL180); identity details that disagree; or a spacecraft emitter category. These are reasons to inspect the data, not claims of impossible aircraft.

Flight patterns

Repeated circling, loitering and repeated passes require several minutes of fresh, plausible positions. Missing altitude or heading does not prevent a match. Noisy or sparse tracks can support only a confined-activity candidate, with a lower confidence level and no reliable circle count. While a verified circle stays within the 20-minute track, turns inside it are not saved again as reversals or loitering, and earlier ones it covers are folded into it. Aircraft are also compared with each other: two flying in close company, three or more circling at different levels, and integrity reports dropping on several aircraft at once. These patterns do not establish a mission.

Special codes

7400, 7500, 7600, 7700 or another transmitted emergency/priority status, US special-use codes, and TCAS resolution advisories that readsb decodes. A sequence such as 7700 then 7600 is one episode. Live findings require two new receiver observations of the same status; isolated archived reports are marked unconfirmed. Code and advisory reports alone do not confirm a real emergency or a near miss.

Evidence and history

Most live findings require two observations with new message counts over at least 8 seconds. Position-jump cards preserve the two positions; isolated extreme archive readings are explicitly marked unconfirmed. Movement findings require two distinct qualifying position endpoints at least 8 seconds apart and are grouped across gaps of up to 2 minutes. Other similar findings for the same address are grouped within 10 minutes of the same live encounter, and identity findings once per aircraft per UTC day. A brief ground report in the middle of a flight does not end the encounter. The journal keeps up to 365 days / 50,000 sightings. Compact replay altitude may be barometric or geometric; current aircraft metadata may differ from the historical aircraft. Imported metric highlights do not preserve complete simultaneous positions or field freshness.

Sources: readsb data fields · FAA transponder guidance · FAA code changes · ICAO type designators
